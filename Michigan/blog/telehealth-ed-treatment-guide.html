How Telehealth for ED Treatment Works: Complete Guide

Telehealth for erectile dysfunction is a private medical visit. A clinician licensed where the patient is located takes a history, decides whether a medicine is appropriate, and sends a prescription to a pharmacy if it is. The visit is not a form that automatically ships a pill.

What the visit is for

Erectile dysfunction is the repeated inability to get or keep an erection sufficient for sex. It has many causes: blood flow, nerve injury, low testosterone, depression, alcohol, and the medicines already being taken for blood pressure or mood. A first visit does not assume the cause is "needs a PDE5 inhibitor." It asks enough to know whether a pill is reasonable or whether something else has to be ruled out first.

The comparison of the two medicines most often prescribed is in tadalafil and sildenafil. The service page is ED treatment.

What the clinician asks

  • How long the problem has been present, and whether it is with every partner and with masturbation.
  • Morning erections, which help separate a physical pattern from a situational one.
  • Heart disease, chest pain, stroke, and any nitrate medicine, including a nitrate used recreationally.
  • Blood pressure treatment, alpha blockers, and any prior PDE5 inhibitor, including the dose and what happened.
  • Diabetes, pelvic surgery, smoking, alcohol, and mood or sleep problems.

A questionnaire can collect those facts. A clinician still has to read them and decide. A service that cannot name that clinician, or that will not refuse anyone, is not doing this visit.

Medicines that may be considered

Sildenafil and tadalafil are the usual first prescriptions. They require sexual stimulation to work, they are unsafe with nitrates, and an erection that has not resolved within four hours is an emergency. Sudden vision or hearing loss is a reason to stop and get care. The dose is the one on the prescription. Ordering a higher strength from a second website, on top of a current prescription, is how people end up in the emergency department.

Testosterone is a different evaluation. It is considered when symptoms and lab results support it, not because an erection was difficult once. A page or a clinic that leads with testosterone for every man with ED is skipping the history above.

When a video visit is the wrong place

Chest pain, pain radiating to the arm or jaw, fainting, or an erection that will not go down are not telehealth problems. They need emergency care. An exam is also the right next step when the history suggests Peyronie's disease that needs to be seen, a genital lesion, or a neurologic deficit. Telehealth can start the workup and can prescribe when the history is enough. It does not replace an exam the clinician says is required.

After the prescription

The prescription goes to a pharmacy the patient chooses. Follow-up is for the people who had side effects, who had no response, or who need a different dose or the other drug in the class. No response to the first fill is common enough that the plan should include a way to report it. It is not a reason to buy a second, unnamed product online.

A real service can also say that medication is not the right treatment. That outcome is part of the visit, not a failure of the website.

What this article does not do

This page explains the decision. It does not approve a medication, set a dose, or replace a clinician who can see the rest of the history. Seek in-person or emergency care for severe or rapidly worsening symptoms.

Who this is for in Michigan

The patients this page is for live in Detroit, Grand Rapids, and Ann Arbor.

This article is for those patients when they are deciding whether telehealth for ED fits a visit with MichiganDoc.

How the MichiganDoc visit applies

The MichiganDoc visit for this topic starts with a structured intake covering symptoms, medications, allergies, and prior treatment response. A Michigan-licensed physician (MI) reviews whether telehealth for ED is appropriate, whether labs are required, and whether another approach is safer. If a prescription is indicated, it is sent to a pharmacy the patient can use in Michigan. That prescription is not a referral to a Toledo or other Ohio clinic across the state line.

Local access

A patient in Detroit, Grand Rapids, and Ann Arbor should compare telehealth for ED with care already available through Henry Ford Health or Michigan Medicine, not with a Toledo or other Ohio clinic across the state line. Labs, when needed, can be drawn through a local partner in Michigan.
